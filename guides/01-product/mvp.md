# MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

> An MVP is the smallest version of a product that lets you learn whether real users get enough value to keep using it — or pay for it.

## What it means

An MVP is not a half-built product and not a demo for investors. It is a learning tool.

The “minimum” part means you strip away features that do not help you test your riskiest assumptions. The “viable” part means the product must still solve a real problem well enough that users get a meaningful outcome.

A good MVP answers questions like:

- Do people have this problem urgently?
- Will they use our solution more than once?
- What do they do instead today?
- Will they pay, or at least take a costly action (time, data, switching)?

Common MVP forms:

| Form | When it helps | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concierge / manual | Demand unclear; automation expensive | Founder personally onboards and delivers the workflow |
| Landing page + waitlist | Messaging and demand unclear | Page explains offer; tracks signup and intent |
| Single-feature product | Core value is clear; scope risk is high | One workflow done excellently |
| Prototype / no-code | UI/UX risk is high | Clickable flow users can attempt |
| Closed beta | Need feedback from a narrow ICP | 20 design partners using a rough build |

## Why it matters

Startups die from building the wrong thing for too long. An MVP reduces that risk by forcing contact with reality early.

Without an MVP mindset, teams often:

- spend months polishing features nobody asked for
- confuse “we shipped code” with “we learned something”
- raise or spend money before knowing what users value

With a strong MVP process, you learn faster than competitors who are still designing the perfect product in private.

## When founders should care

- **Day 0 to first customers:** almost always. You need a way to test demand.
- **Before a big rewrite or new product line:** treat the new bet like an MVP again.
- **After early traction:** the MVP phase ends. Do not keep using “MVP” as an excuse for a broken core experience once users rely on you.

If you already have clear [product-market fit](product-market-fit.md), obsessing over “more MVP” can become an excuse to underinvest in reliability, onboarding, and expansion.

## How it works

A practical MVP loop:

1. **State the assumption.** Example: “Ops managers at mid-market e-commerce companies will pay $200/mo to automate returns triage.”
2. **Define the success signal.** Example: 10 of 20 design partners complete the weekly workflow without founder help; 5 convert to paid.
3. **Cut scope ruthlessly.** Keep only what tests the assumption.
4. **Ship to real [ICP](../02-customers/icp.md) users**, not friends who will be polite.
5. **Measure behavior**, not compliments. Usage, retention, payment, and referrals beat survey praise.
6. **Decide:** persist, pivot the offer, or kill the idea.

### What “viable” looks like

Viable usually means the user can complete a valuable job:

- get a report they trust
- send an invoice
- book a meeting
- generate a draft they edit and ship
- complete a purchase flow

If users cannot get an outcome, you built a prototype, not an MVP.

## Practical example

A two-founder SaaS team wants to build an AI tool for customer support teams.

**Too big first version:** full inbox, AI agent, analytics suite, Slack app, SSO, role permissions.

**Better MVP:**

- connect to one helpdesk export (CSV or Zendesk)
- auto-draft replies for the top 10 ticket types
- human agent edits and sends
- track edit rate and time saved

**Targets for 6 weeks:**

- 15 support teams try it
- median agent edits less than 30% of draft content
- at least 5 teams use it weekly for 3+ weeks
- 3 teams pay $150–$400/mo

If agents rewrite every draft, the product is not viable yet — even if the UI looks polished.

## Common confusion

| Term | Difference from MVP |
| --- | --- |
| Prototype | Shows how something *might* work; may not deliver real outcomes |
| Beta | Often implies a near-complete product being stabilized |
| MLP (Minimum Lovable Product) | Emphasizes delight; useful later, risky if it delays learning |
| V1 | A production release; may be larger than an MVP |
| Smoke test | Tests interest (ads/landing pages); may not test product value |

Also: “MVP” does not mean “embarrassing quality forever.” Ship rough edges on non-critical parts. Do not ship something unsafe, untrustworthy, or useless.
