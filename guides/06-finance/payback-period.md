# Payback Period

> CAC payback period is how many months it takes for a customer’s cumulative gross profit to repay the cost of acquiring them.

## What it means

```text
Payback (months) ≈ CAC / (ARPU × Gross margin %)
```

Example:

- CAC = $1,200
- ARPU = $200
- Gross margin = 80% → $160/month gross profit
- Payback = 1,200 / 160 = **7.5 months**

This ignores churn during payback and expansion; more precise models use cohort cash contribution curves.

## Why it matters

A company can have an attractive LTV:CAC and still die from cash timing if payback is very long and growth is fast. Payback connects unit economics to [runway](runway.md).

## When founders should care

- Before accelerating acquisition spend
- When choosing SMB vs enterprise focus
- In board financial planning
- When capital markets tighten (shorter payback becomes more valuable)

## How it works

### What “good” looks like

Depends on model and capital conditions. Many SaaS teams aim for roughly <12 months fully loaded payback as a comfortable zone, with some enterprise motions accepting longer. Treat ranges as context, not commandments.

### Relationship to growth

Faster growth with long payback consumes more cash. That can be fine if funded and retention is proven — or fatal if not.

## Practical example

Enterprise motion:

- CAC $18,000
- ARPU $2,000/mo
- margin 85% → $1,700/mo
- payback ≈ 10.6 months

SMB motion:

- CAC $300
- ARPU $50/mo
- margin 80% → $40/mo
- payback = 7.5 months

Enterprise may still be better if retention/expansion is stronger — compare full systems, not payback alone.

## Common confusion

Payback is not the same as break-even for the whole company. It is a per-customer acquisition recovery metric.
